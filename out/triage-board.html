Why blocks

One doorway calling on its own wants a bollard or a driveway marking. A block where nine doorways are all still calling is one problem, not nine, and nine signs is the wrong answer to it. Blocks here are HRM's Census 2021 Dissemination Areas, which carry a dwelling count, so a block's load is shown per 1,000 dwellings as well as raw. Dense blocks call more; the rate is what separates a real problem from a lot of front doors.

Three coarser groupings were tried and thrown out. The community field on the call record is useless: 7,651 of 9,791 driveway calls just say HALIFAX. HRM's Community Boundaries layer fails the same way. Community Plan Areas has only 22 polygons for the whole municipality.

The map

Drawn from HRM's own Street Network layer, 10,599 street segments, and from the census block outlines. It is vector, not tiles, so it works with no external service and zooms without pixelating. Street names appear as you zoom in.

Read these numbers carefully

HRM publishes no ticketing field. A tow is the only enforcement outcome in the data, so a call with no tow is a call with no recorded outcome, not proof that nothing happened. The tow comparison is observational: tows may cluster at the worst addresses, which would hide a real effect. Vehicle identity is make, model and colour, not a plate, so two identical cars count as one and the unique count is a floor. Addresses are matched as text, not geocoded, so one doorway can split across two rows. A block's street label is derived from the doorways on this list, not from an official name, and its dwelling count covers the whole block rather than the street the calls sit on.
